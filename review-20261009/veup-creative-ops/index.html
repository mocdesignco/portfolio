Giving creative work a better starting point

Too much creative time was being spent gathering missing information and chasing handoffs. I redesigned the routes for planned content and ad hoc requests, so the work could start with a usable brief and move through clear decisions.

02 / VeUP Creative & Content Operations

Before the design came the detective work

Requests often arrived without the audience, objective or technical context needed to begin. For a typical three-page service overview, I estimated that gathering the missing information could take around six hours.

Writing, design, review and publishing still had to happen after that. Improving the process meant looking closely at what surrounded production, including the time spent making a request usable in the first place.

03 / VeUP Creative & Content Operations

Two kinds of work needed different treatment

Recurring content could be planned backwards from publication. Ad hoc creative requests needed a way to assess missing information, priority and capacity before deciding who should deliver them.

I designed separate, connected routes for each, with clear ownership and review stages. Both kept human creative oversight in the process.

04 / VeUP Creative & Content Operations

Turning business priorities into a content plan

I developed and coordinated a pipeline for technical articles, LinkedIn content, lead magnets, newsletters, sales collateral and event creative. Leadership defined the business priorities and service focus; I translated them into planned content and delivery.

I coordinated input and technical review from subject-matter experts, briefed external writers and designers, and retained final creative oversight.

A six-week workback gave each stage a place in the schedule, from technical input and writing through review, design, revisions and publication checks. It made dependencies visible while there was still time to do something about them.

05 / VeUP Creative & Content Operations

Giving incoming requests a usable route

For immediate needs such as service overviews and sales collateral, I introduced structured intake, an information-completeness check and a capacity assessment before production.

That gave me a clearer basis for prioritising work and deciding whether to deliver it in-house or involve external support. Review, approval, publishing and knowledge capture followed within the same process.

06 / VeUP Creative & Content Operations

Connecting the tools around the work

I built the production layer in Notion, linking each item to its publication target, stage, owner, dependencies, content pillar and reviewers.

Make, Slack, Tally, email, APIs and AI connected repetitive handoffs. Notifications and stage changes helped work move between people, while creative judgement, technical review and approval remained human responsibilities.

I initiated the work from within the delivery process, mapping the friction and building the workflows, SOPs and capacity logic around it.

What changed in the ad hoc workflow

For a standard three-page service overview, the illustrative comparison was:

MeasureBeforeRedesigned workflow
Estimated calendar turnaround2–3 weeks1–2 days
Estimated hands-on effort11–12 hours2–3 hours

Estimated reduction in hands-on effort: Approximately 75–80%.

Estimates are based on a standard three-page service overview and the workflow stages described. Calendar turnaround and hands-on effort are separate measures.

The practical aim was to give creative time a better use than reconstructing briefs and chasing status.
